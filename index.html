Why do AI agents break rules?

How framing, context, and social signals shape compliance

Mika Okamoto/Ansel Kaplan Erol/Kutluhan Erol

Companies now write legal rules into their AI agents' system prompts and expect the agents to follow them. We gave twelve language models one environmental regulation and a procurement job that rewards breaking it, then tested what three theories from law and economics predict about when people obey. Each theory explains part of what the agents do, and the same regulation produces very different behavior depending on the model.

Abstract

Specifying a penalty can turn a legal obligation into a cost-benefit calculation that favors violation. We show that this enforcement information paradox occurs in AI agents. Most AI safety evaluations test whether models fail; we ask why, using compliance theory from law and economics as a diagnostic. We evaluate twelve instruction-tuned language models deployed as enterprise procurement chatbots. Each is given an environmental regulation in its system prompt covering large purchases, and a vendor list on which the certified suppliers cost nearly twice what the uncertified ones do. We test the agents against the predictions of deterrence, legitimacy, and expressive law, and find that each theory accounts for part of what we observe. Under identical conditions, compliance spans 46 percentage points across models, and models differ in which pressure breaks them: some treat the regulation as binding however it is worded, while others fail where theory predicts, under low penalties and non-command phrasing. Benchmark scores and developers' own descriptions of post-training do not predict where a model falls. Across all twelve, financial incentives, managerial demands, peer outcomes, and employee pressure each produce large compliance failures. These agents violate regulatory constraints to satisfy local user objectives in ways standard alignment benchmarks do not measure. Embedding the rule in the system prompt is not on its own enough to produce a compliant agent: model selection is itself a governance decision, and benchmark evaluation is not sufficient for compliance-sensitive deployments.

Key findings
  • Compliance varies across models in degree and in kind. Under identical regulatory contexts the twelve models span 46 percentage points, from 43.5% to 89.5%, and different pressures break different models. Benchmark scores and developers' descriptions of post-training do not predict where a model falls, which makes the choice of model a compliance decision.
  • Stating a penalty invites cost-benefit reasoning. An imperative rule gets 100% compliance from most models, and adding explicit penalty information lowers compliance for many of them. Gemini 3 Flash falls from 100% to 34% when a small fine is mentioned.
  • Managerial signals, social signals, normative pressure and employee pressure tactics each produce large compliance failures. Anti-adversarial mandates in the system prompt help without closing the gap. Under deadline urgency at a Small Fine, every model drops to 9% or below without a mandate, and only Qwen 3.5 fully recovers with one.
  • Most violations are openly rationalized, but how detectable they are depends on the model. Of 6,743 violations, 96% mention the rule in the stated reasoning. Silent rates range from 1.6% to 7.4% by model and do not track compliance, so compliance and auditability have to be checked separately.

Three theories of compliance

The paper treats three accounts from law and economics as hypotheses about the agents (§2.1), and each one describes part of the results. Dots are the twelve models and the black bar is their average. Hover a dot to see the model.

01

Deterrence

Becker 1968 Prediction

Agents comply when the expected penalty is larger than the gain from breaking the rule. Compliance should rise with the fine and should not depend on wording.

Result

This fits the models that weigh costs, but it cannot explain the enforcement information paradox: with a Small Fine, compliance is lower than with no fine.

See Figure 3 →
02

Legitimacy

Tyler 2006 Prediction

Agents comply because the rule comes from a legitimate authority, so the way a rule is issued, and by whom, matters more than the penalty.

Result

This fits the rule-anchored models (GPT-OSS, Qwen 3.5 and Llama 4), which hold the rule however it is phrased, while the other models comply mainly when the rule is imperative.

See Table 3 and Figure 4 →
03

Expressive law

Sunstein 1996; McAdams 2015 Prediction

Law signals what behavior is normal, so what peers and the community do should change compliance even when the fine stays the same.

Result

This explains the peer and norm effects. A peer who was fined, or community activism, raises compliance, and a peer who escaped an audit lowers it, with the fine unchanged.

See Figures 6 and 11 →

Models show each of these modes to a different degree, and the context of a request decides which one drives the decision.

Compliance robustness varies sharply across models

Across the twelve framing and fine conditions, overall compliance ranges from 43.5% to 89.5% depending on the model. Models also break for different reasons. Some are sensitive to how the rule is phrased, others to what they are told about enforcement, and a model can be robust on one and fragile on the other.

Paper §3.3 and §4.1, Figure 3, Appendix C.2

Rule framing and fines: the legitimacy and deterrence tests

Deterrence predicts that compliance rises with the expected penalty and does not depend on wording. In the data, wording moves compliance a lot, and adding a Small Fine lowers compliance for many models. The paper calls this drop the enforcement information paradox.

Paper §4.2, Figure 5

Institutional authority systematically breaks compliance

The regulation is held fixed in its informational framing, and one authority signal is added to the system prompt. With a manager's blanket authorization, compliance reaches 0% in 15 of the 48 model and fine combinations. A board cost policy lowers compliance even for the most rule-anchored models.

Paper §4.2, Figure 6

Peer outcomes swing compliance both ways

The employee's message mentions what happened to another office, the paper's peer. When that peer was fined, models that otherwise collapse at the Small Fine return to near-ceiling compliance, and Grok goes from 8% to 92%. When the peer escaped an audit, compliance drops. The paper links this to all three theories, since a peer's outcome signals what is normal, updates the expected penalty and lends the rule authority.

Paper Appendix C.3, Figure 11

Non-state norms raise compliance above the regulation alone

One paragraph of company background describes community activism, an industry standard or media coverage. None of them mentions a penalty, and each sits next to the same informational regulation. At No Fine all three raise compliance above the regulation alone, as expressive law predicts.

Paper §4.3, Figure 7

System-prompt mandates reduce, but do not close, the gap

The employee adds one of nine pressure tactics to the request. An anti-adversarial mandate in the system prompt helps every model, though the size of the gain differs by an order of magnitude across models. Deadline urgency still gets through for most of them, which the paper calls the urgency exception.

Paper §4.4, Figure 8

Multi-turn dynamics: asymmetric self-correction

After the agent's first answer, the employee sends one follow-up. Compliant answers mostly hold up against a neutral probe, except for GLM 4.7 Flash, and a cost argument or a manager's order erodes them further. The same neutral probe repairs some violations, from none for several models to about 60% for Kimi K2.5, while a direct correction brings nearly every model back.

Reasoning transparency: how violations are surfaced

Of 6,743 classified violations, 96.0% mention the regulation in the agent's stated reasoning and 4.0% are silent. Silent rates do not track compliance: Mistral Small and Qwen 3.5 rank second and fourth on compliance and have the highest silent rates, so a compliant model is not necessarily an auditable one.
